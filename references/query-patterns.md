# Query Patterns That Work

## Why this matters

Search engines reward specificity. Generic queries surface marketing content. Specific queries surface the actual friction. These patterns have been tested against real product research and consistently surface verbatim user complaints.

## The four query types

### 1. Source-anchored

Pin the search to a known signal-rich source.

- `[product] cons site:capterra.com`
- `[product] dislike site:g2.com`
- `[product] reviews site:reddit.com`
- `[product] site:appstore.com` (rarely indexed well, prefer the App Store directly)
- `[product] site:trustradius.com`

### 2. Sentiment-anchored

Pin the search to negative sentiment words. Each of these surfaces a different shape of complaint.

- `[product] frustrating` — emotional friction, usually IA or onboarding
- `[product] confusing` — IA, terminology, mental model mismatch
- `[product] broken` — actual bugs and reliability issues
- `[product] slow` — performance
- `[product] missing` — feature gaps
- `[product] crashes` — mobile reliability
- `[product] expensive` — pricing/value perception
- `[product] hate` — strong sentiment, but lots of noise. Use sparingly.
- `[product] difficult to use` — usability
- `[product] doesn't work` — broad reliability

### 3. Workflow-anchored

How users describe the work they're trying to do.

- `[product] how to [common task]` — if a task needs a tutorial, it's a friction point
- `[product] tutorial [common task]` — same signal, different phrasing
- `[product] workaround` — gold. Workarounds reveal exact product gaps.
- `[product] alternative to [feature]` — what users are leaving for
- `I switched from [product] to` — competitive churn stories

### 4. Comparative

Surfaces what people leave for and why.

- `[product] vs [main competitor]` — head-to-head, usually reveals tradeoffs honestly
- `[product] alternatives` — the alternatives list itself tells you what the product is missing
- `why I left [product]` — strong churn signal
- `[product] vs [competitor] reddit` — less marketed, more honest

## What not to search

- `[product] is bad` — surfaces SEO-bait, not real users
- `[product] sucks` — same
- `[product] review` alone — surfaces only marketing content and incentivized reviews
- Generic questions like `is [product] good` — wastes search budget

## Multi-product queries

When researching a product with strong competitors, you'll often find better signal in comparative content than in single-product reviews. People are more honest about tradeoffs than about a single product they're either still using (positivity bias) or no longer using (sour-grapes bias).

## When to read the marketing site

After the friction pass, not before. Reading marketing first anchors you to the product team's framing and makes you less likely to notice the gap between promise and reality. Read it last, after you already know what users actually experience.

## When to read the help center

Help centers reveal what the team has had to write a doc about. Common articles are often friction tells:
- "How to [obvious task]" articles → IA or affordance issue
- "Troubleshooting [thing]" articles → reliability issue
- "Why is [thing] [unexpected behavior]" → product surprises users

Look for "Popular Articles" or "Most Viewed" sorting if available.

## Time-boxing

Spend no more than 2 minutes per query. If a query doesn't surface useful signal, move on. Don't try to make a bad query work by adding modifiers.

Total search time across all queries: 15 to 20 minutes maximum. Synthesis is where the value is, not collection.
